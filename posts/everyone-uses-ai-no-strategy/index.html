Everyone Uses AI. Almost Nobody Has a Strategy.

By Mari Anuashvili Mon, Sep 28, 2026

Pretty much every product manager uses AI now, but how many of their companies actually have a strategy for it? Not many. Productside surveyed more than 250 product professionals this year and found that about four in five use AI regularly, but only one in four work somewhere with an actual strategy behind it.

The results reflect that. A widely cited 2025 MIT report found that about 95% of enterprise generative AI pilots produced no measurable return. The study's methods have drawn fair criticism, and other research is more optimistic, but the number spread because it matched what a lot of people were seeing at work: plenty of activity, not much to show for it.

Why the gap exists

Most AI use at work is private. A PM finds a prompt that turns messy interview notes into a clean summary, uses it every week, and never mentions it. The PM on the next team is paying for a different tool and writing her own prompts from scratch. Nobody compares notes, so a good idea stays with one person.

Nobody measures it either. Ask a team how much time AI saves them and you'll usually get a shrug or a guess. Without numbers, leadership can't tell which uses are worth scaling, and AI stays a personal habit instead of something the company can build on.

There's also a pattern in what people use it for. PMs have adopted AI almost universally for writing: specs, PRDs, meeting summaries. Far fewer use it to analyze customer feedback, even though that's where AI actually shapes a decision instead of a document. Left alone, people drift toward whatever is easiest, not whatever matters most.

Why this is product ops' job

It's tempting to say each PM should just get better at AI. But PMs are measured on shipping product, and "build a system for the whole team" never makes it to the top of their list. It shouldn't have to.

Product ops already owns the pieces this problem is made of. Gocious, a roadmap software company, describes three jobs product ops plays in AI adoption: keeping data organized and accessible, standardizing the tools teams use, and making sure information about what's working flows back to PMs. Those are the same levers you'd pull to turn scattered personal use into real results.

What it looks like in practice

A shared prompt library. The prompts that work get saved in one place, with a note on what each one is for and who maintains it. When someone improves a prompt, everyone benefits. It's the AI version of a team template, and it's the cheapest win available.

A standard set of tools. Product ops picks a small set of approved tools and sets them up properly, with access to the right data and clear rules about what can and can't go into them. That cuts duplicate spending and makes it possible to compare results across teams.

Measuring what matters. Counting how many people use AI tells you almost nothing. Better questions: How many hours did the team get back this month? Did we analyze more customer feedback than last quarter? Did any decision change because of what we found? Those are the numbers that make the case to leadership, and product ops has the access and the mandate to track them.

Why I care about this

I came to product from two directions. I started as a financial analyst, where every investment had to show a return, and then trained as a software engineer, where I built products on top of AI APIs. The finance side of me notices the gap between spending and results first. The engineering side knows most of these fixes aren't technically hard. The hard part is coordination, and coordination is exactly what product ops is for.

If you work in product ops, I'd love to hear what's worked on your team. What's one thing you did that turned AI from an individual habit into a team result?
